### Preliminary Results

The pilot asks two questions: does FedVerify reach high accuracy when a fifth of the clients are adversarial, and what does that cost in training time and communication? We ran it on a 100-node heterogeneous edge testbed, training [MODEL] on [DATASET]. Twenty of the 100 clients were Byzantine (adversarial participants that may deviate arbitrarily from the protocol); here they sent poisoned updates meant to degrade the shared model. Client data were non-IID, meaning the class mix differs from one client to the next. We partitioned the data with a Dirichlet distribution at alpha = 0.2; smaller alpha concentrates each client's data on fewer classes, so 0.2 produces strong skew. This combination is a hard case for robust aggregation: skewed data makes honest updates disagree with one another, so poisoned updates are harder to single out.

We compare FedVerify against three baselines. FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017) averages client updates with no defense. Krum (Blanchard et al., 2017) selects the single update closest to its nearest neighbors and discards the rest. FL-Trust (Cao et al., 2021) computes a server update on a small clean dataset and weights each client update by how closely its direction matches the server's.

Without a defense, the 20 Byzantine clients stall training: FedAvg never reaches 80% accuracy and finishes at 34.2% (Table 4). All three defenses pass 80%. Against FL-Trust (83.1%) and Krum (81.0%), FedVerify gains 2.3 and 4.4 points, for a final accuracy of 85.4%.

The largest gains are in convergence speed. Each round consists of local training on the clients followed by one aggregation step on the server. To reach 80% accuracy, Krum needs 340 rounds and FL-Trust needs 210; FedVerify needs 115. In wall-clock time, FedVerify reaches 80% in 5.6 hours, against 14.8 hours for Krum and 11.2 hours for FL-Trust: speedups of 2.6× and 2.0×. Fewer rounds account for this gain. Wall-clock time divided by rounds gives a per-round average of 2.9 minutes for FedVerify, between Krum (2.6 minutes) and FL-Trust (3.2 minutes). FedVerify thus spends about 12% more time per round than Krum, but its nearly threefold reduction in rounds outweighs that overhead.

FedVerify sends 14.1 MB per round, 1.7 MB (14%) more than FedAvg and Krum and 4.1 MB (23%) less than FL-Trust. For devices on metered or low-bandwidth links, the relevant budget is total traffic, which also depends on how many rounds each defense needs. Multiplying per-round communication by rounds to 80% accuracy gives each defense's total cost of reaching the target. FedVerify's total is about 1.6 GB, 58% below FL-Trust (3.8 GB) and 62% below Krum (4.2 GB).

These results come from a single configuration (one testbed, 20% Byzantine clients, alpha = 0.2), and Table 4 reports no run-to-run variance. We therefore treat the 2.3-point accuracy margin over FL-Trust as preliminary. The 2.0× wall-clock speedup and 58% communication reduction are larger effects, but they also need confirmation across random seeds and settings. Three questions remain open: how FedVerify behaves at Byzantine fractions above 20%, against adaptive attackers who know FedVerify's design, and under weaker or stronger data skew. These questions motivate the proposed research.
