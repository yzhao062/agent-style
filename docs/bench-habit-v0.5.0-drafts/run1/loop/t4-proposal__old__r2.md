### Preliminary Results

We piloted FedVerify on a 100-node heterogeneous edge testbed to measure its resistance to model poisoning on diverse edge hardware. FedVerify's [verification mechanism], described in Section [X], targets poisoning under heterogeneous client data, and the pilot tests that setting. Twenty of the 100 clients (20%) acted as Byzantine clients, which send corrupted model updates to degrade the shared model. Client data were non-IID (not independent and identically distributed), simulated with a Dirichlet label partition at concentration alpha = 0.2 (Hsu et al. 2019). A smaller alpha produces more skewed client label distributions. We evaluated [model] on [dataset] using [hardware]. Byzantine clients applied [attack and parameters]. Section [X] documents client sampling, optimization settings, data partitions, and evaluation.

The baselines are FedAvg (McMahan et al. 2017), Krum (Blanchard et al. 2017), and FL-Trust (Cao et al. 2021). FedAvg averages all client updates with no defense; Krum and FL-Trust are Byzantine-robust aggregation rules. FL-Trust also requires a clean root dataset held by the server. The pilot used a root dataset of [size and composition], and its server-side training cost is [included in / excluded from] the wall-clock measurements. Section [X] specifies each method's configuration and resource assumptions, including any server-held data.

Table 4 reports [individual-run results / means across N runs] for four measures per method: rounds to 80% accuracy, wall-clock time to 80% accuracy, final accuracy, and communication per round. Communication per round counts [client or system scope] and includes [update, verification, and other counted traffic].

FedAvg did not reach 80% accuracy within [training budget] and finished at 34.2%. FedVerify reached 80% accuracy in 115 rounds and 5.6 hours. FL-Trust needed 210 rounds and 11.2 hours, and Krum needed 340 rounds and 14.8 hours. In these measurements, FedVerify used approximately 45% fewer rounds and 50% less wall-clock time than FL-Trust, the faster of the two robust baselines. Against Krum, it used approximately 66% fewer rounds and 62% less wall-clock time.

Average round times to the 80% threshold were 2.6 minutes for Krum, 2.9 for FedVerify, and 3.2 for FL-Trust. Fewer rounds drove the speedup over both baselines. Against FL-Trust, shorter rounds added to the gain. Krum's rounds were shorter than FedVerify's, which narrows the wall-clock gap (62%) relative to the round gap (66%).

Final accuracies are measured at [each method's stopping criterion and training budget], which were [equal / unequal] across methods. At those endpoints, FedVerify ended highest at 85.4%, 2.3 points above FL-Trust (83.1%) and 4.4 points above Krum (81.0%).

FedVerify sends 14.1 MB per round. That is 1.7 MB (14%) above the 12.4 MB of FedAvg and Krum, and 4.1 MB (23%) below the 18.2 MB of FL-Trust. Multiplying per-round communication by rounds to 80% accuracy gives estimated totals of 1.62 GB for FedVerify, 3.82 GB for FL-Trust, and 4.22 GB for Krum. These estimates assume constant communication per round and use decimal gigabytes.

Table 4 gives one value per metric and no confidence intervals, so uncertainty in these differences remains unquantified. The pilot also covers a single Byzantine fraction (20%), a single heterogeneity level (alpha = 0.2), and poisoning attacks only. Other Byzantine fractions, other heterogeneity levels, and other attack strategies remain untested. The pilot supports the feasibility of FedVerify in the tested configuration; attributing the observed gains to individual components requires ablation experiments.
